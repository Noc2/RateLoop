# RateLoop tokenless — legal position

Written 29 July 2026. Legal claims are cited to primary sources. This is a
working analysis for deciding what the product may claim; it is not legal advice
and several items below are flagged for counsel.

**Freshness warning.** The governing amendment to the AI Act entered into force on
**27 July 2026**, two days before this was written. Most commentary in circulation
predates it.

---

## 1. EU AI Act — where things actually stand

**Regulation (EU) 2026/1744** of 8 July 2026, amending Regulation (EU) 2024/1689,
was published on 24 July 2026 and entered into force on 27 July 2026.

| Provision                                                | Applies from                              |
| -------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------- |
| Prohibitions, definitions                                | 2 Feb 2025 — in force                     |
| General-purpose AI obligations, penalties                | 2 Aug 2025 — in force, **not delayed**    |
| Transparency (Art. 50)                                   | 2 Aug 2026                                |
| **Annex III high-risk (employment, credit, education…)** | **2 Dec 2027 — deferred from 2 Aug 2026** |
| Embedded high-risk (Annex I)                             | 2 Aug 2028                                |

Article 4 on AI literacy was replaced entirely: the duty softened from _ensuring_ a
level of literacy to _taking measures to support_ it.

Articles 12, 14, 26 and 99 appear unamended.

### Article 14 binds the provider, not the deployer

This is the finding that most changes how the product should talk.

Article 14(1): high-risk systems _"shall be designed and developed"_ so they can be
effectively overseen. 14(3): oversight measures are identified and built **by the
provider**. 14(4) opens: the system _"shall be provided to the deployer"_ such that
overseers are enabled.

**Article 14 is a provider design-and-provision obligation.** The deployer's
counterpart is Article 26. Product pages currently structure themselves around "the
five Article 14(4) measures" paired with "you remain responsible for…" addressed to
a deployer. That is a misattribution.

The saving nuance — probably true of most of the target market — is that a company
building its own agent and using it is **both provider and deployer**, so Article 14
does bind it. The pages should say so rather than assume it.

**What Article 14 does not require:** review of every output; any particular number
of reviewers (except for one biometric category, which requires two); independence
from the deployer; external or blinded review; any evidence artefact, signature, log
format or attestation; or that reviewers be domain experts.

**There is therefore no Article 14 requirement that RateLoop uniquely satisfies.**
What RateLoop supplies is _how a deployer chooses to organise_ oversight, plus
evidence that it did.

### Article 26 — the deployer's actual duties

26(2): assign oversight to natural persons with **competence, training and
authority**. 26(3): this is without prejudice to _"the deployer's freedom to
organise its own resources and activities"_. 26(6): keep logs generated by the
system and under the deployer's control for at least six months. 26(7): employers
must inform workers.

**On 26(6):** the six-month floor attaches to logs the _AI system_ generates under
the _deployer's_ control. RateLoop's review records are not those logs. A six-month
retention default is sensible product behaviour; it is not that obligation being
met.

### Article 12 — record-keeping

A **provider** design-capability duty. The only fields it names apply to one
biometric category and have no bearing on a text-output review product. Listing
Article 12 in a deployer-facing mapping is an error.

### Can RateLoop discharge a customer's obligation?

**It can carry out the work. It cannot assume the obligation.** The distinction is
textual, not merely prudential.

1. **The obligation cannot transfer — certain.** The Act provides a mechanism for a
   third party to acquire _provider_ status (Art. 25(1)) and **no equivalent
   mechanism for deployer status at all**. Penalties fall on the deployer. No
   contract moves that.

2. **Performing the oversight task through a third party is lawful — strong, not
   settled.** Article 26(2) says "natural persons", **not employees**, and 26(3)
   expressly preserves the deployer's freedom to organise its resources. Recital 91
   speaks of "the persons assigned". _Caveat: no Commission guidance, authority
   decision or harmonised standard confirms this yet, and none exists — the
   high-risk regime does not apply until December 2027. Present it as a defensible
   reading, not as settled law._

3. **The binding constraint is "authority", and it decides the product's shape.** A
   stranger on a public reviewer network has no authority over the customer's
   system and cannot be an Article 26(2) oversight person, however competent.

This produces a split the product should adopt explicitly:

| Lane                                     | Article 26(2) oversight?                                                                            | What it is                                                                         |
| ---------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Invited** — customer-designated people | **Potentially yes**, if the customer designates them and confers competence, training and authority | Instrumentation of the customer's own oversight                                    |
| **Public paid network**                  | **No** — no authority, no designation                                                               | An independent quality signal; valuable, but not oversight in the Article 26 sense |

One existing sentence already draws this line correctly, and it is buried at the
bottom of one page. **It should be the organising frame of the whole legal story.**
It is also commercially stronger, because it tells a buyer which lane to buy for
which purpose.

### Roles, and penalties

RateLoop is **none of the Act's regulated operator roles** with respect to a
customer's AI system — not provider, deployer, importer or distributor. Customers
are deployers, and often also providers.

Article 25(1) lists the only ways a third party becomes a provider: putting its name
on the system, substantially modifying it, or changing its intended purpose so it
becomes high-risk. **Nothing in the current design triggers these** — provided
RateLoop never white-labels a customer's system, and provided a future "verified
host" adapter that intercepts and alters outputs is not characterised as a
substantial modification. That second risk is real and worth watching.

**Article 25(4) is RateLoop's actual statutory foothold, and the product never
mentions it.** Providers of high-risk systems are _required_ to have written
agreements with third parties supplying tools, services, components or processes.
That is precisely RateLoop's position — a cooperation duty rather than an operator
role, and a commercially useful hook, because providers need such agreements.

Penalties: up to €35m or 7% for prohibited practices; **up to €15m or 3% for
deployer obligations including Article 26**. For SMEs the _lower_ of the amount or
the percentage applies — which is RateLoop's own tier.

### The exposure this section originally missed: RateLoop's own reviewers

Everything above concerns the customer's AI system. The sharper question is whether
RateLoop operates a high-risk system **of its own**, and the answer turns on a single
architectural fact.

Annex III(4)(b) covers systems used "to allocate tasks based on individual behaviour
or personal traits" and "to monitor and evaluate the performance and behaviour of
persons" in work relationships. RateLoop scores its own reviewers, routes work by
those scores, and can pause assignments. Recital 57 puts the point beyond argument
for this product: the category expressly reaches **access to self-employment** and
persons providing services through platforms, so "our reviewers are contractors" is
not a defence.

**It escapes today for exactly one reason: none of it is an AI system.** Reviewer
quality is Krippendorff's alpha and dissent rates; assignment is seeded hash ranking
with rule-based filtering; sampling is HMAC. Article 3(1) requires a system that
_infers_, and the Commission's February 2025 guidelines place rule-based software
outside the definition. Chapter III is never reached.

Two things follow, and they are the most consequential constraints in this document.

**The deterministic design is load-bearing.** It is not merely elegant; it is what
keeps RateLoop out of the Act. Any inference introduced into scoring, routing or
triage puts the product inside Article 3(1) — and because classification is assessed
on the system as placed on the market, an inference feature anywhere plausibly makes
the whole product an AI system, at which point reviewer management is Annex III(4)(b)
with RateLoop as **provider**, carrying the full Article 16 stack.

**The Article 6(3) escape hatch is unavailable.** It disapplies high-risk status for
narrow procedural or preparatory tasks — but a proviso states that an Annex III
system is _always_ high-risk where it performs profiling of natural persons, and a
persistent per-reviewer quality score is the textbook case. Note also that claiming
the filter is not free: Article 6(4) requires a documented pre-market assessment and
Article 49(2) requires registration in the EU database. Both survived the Digital
Omnibus, contrary to some commentary describing the rejected proposal.

### Article 50 — the obligation that is not deferred

Article 50 transparency applies from **2 August 2026** and was untouched by the
deferral. If RateLoop ever generates content — an LLM summary of a candidate output
for a reviewer is the realistic case — then 50(2) requires machine-readable marking
of synthetic output, and 50(1) requires telling a person they are interacting with an
AI system. Article 111(4) gives generative systems already on the market before that
date until 2 December 2026 to meet the marking requirement.

Conversely, **50(4) is an asset the product does not use.** The disclosure duty for
AI-generated text published to inform the public does not apply where the content
underwent human review and a person holds editorial responsibility. That is a
verbatim description of what RateLoop sells.

### eIDAS — the evidence has no presumption, and the fix is cheap

Signed evidence is admissible and nothing more. Articles 25(1), 35(1) and 41(1)
guarantee only that electronic form is not itself a ground for exclusion.

The presumptions live one tier up and all require a qualified trust service provider:
**Article 41(2)** gives a qualified timestamp a presumption of accurate time and
integrity; **Article 35(2)** gives a qualified seal a presumption of integrity and
correct origin; and **Article 45k(2)**, added in 2024, gives a qualified electronic
ledger a presumption of unique sequential ordering and integrity. National law hangs
its own presumptions off that same tier — German ZPO § 371a and French Code civil
art. 1367 both key to the qualified variant.

An advanced seal and a qualified seal can be cryptographically identical. The
difference is institutional, and it is the whole difference: without it, **the
customer bears the burden of proving hash construction, key custody and clock source
— which puts RateLoop in the witness box in its customer's litigation.**

RateLoop today signs with a software Ed25519 key held in an environment variable,
about its own records. That is self-attestation with no independent anchor, and the
word eIDAS appears nowhere in the codebase.

The remedy is a procurement decision rather than an engineering project: **ETSI EN
319 422 is a profile of RFC 3161, which the attestation pipeline already
implements.** Qualified timestamps run roughly €0.50–2.50 each. Becoming a QTSP is
the thing to avoid — conformity assessment, supervisory approval and biennial audits,
with listing being constitutive.

## 2. The claims the product currently makes

### A structural gap first

The `/legal/` tree makes **no AI Act claims at all**. Every AI Act claim sits in
`/docs/` and on the homepage. **The Terms therefore do not govern, qualify or
disclaim the regulatory representations that actually sell the product.**

### Claims stronger than the law supports

Ranked by exposure.

1. **"Verified host" enforcement is offered as the answer to Article 14(4)(e), and
   no host holds that tier.** The capability registry shows every host as supported
   or experimental; the module's own comment says no host is verified; the type
   system pins the verified variant off. Yet the docs say a verified host _"can
   establish that an eligible output stayed undelivered"_, _"can prove the output
   stayed blocked"_, and — flatly contradicting the registry — _"Codex desktop is
   the primary verified path."_

   **This is the most serious item in the set**: an unavailable capability offered
   as the answer to the one Article 14 measure the product cannot otherwise
   support, using the verb _prove_. The existing tier-honesty test covers only the
   connection pages, which is why this language survived elsewhere. It is exposed
   under misleading-advertising law as much as under the AI Act.

2. **Two incompatible definitions of "verified".** The host guide defines it as a
   release-gated smoke test at a named client version — a QA milestone that says
   nothing about delivery control. The oversight pages use it to mean _enforces
   withheld delivery_. Same word, two meanings, one of them regulatory.

3. **Articles 12 and 72 listed in a deployer-facing mapping.** Both are provider
   obligations.

4. **The Article 14(4) card structure is itself a compliance checklist** —
   requirement citation paired one-to-one with a feature, five times. Each card is
   hedged; the _form_ is the claim.

5. **A US securities record-keeping rule appears as a mapped row.** That rule
   imposes system-level requirements a review vendor structurally cannot supply.
   Delete the row rather than hedge it.

6. **Article 26(6) answered with "a six-month retention floor"**, which implies a
   sufficiency that does not hold (see §1).

7. **No "not legal advice" disclaimer on any docs page.** It exists only inside a
   downloadable machine-readable file. For a product organised around statutory
   citations, that is conspicuous.

8. **A wallet-creation capability described in the present tense** while
   configuration mandates it off and fail-closed in hosted environments.

### Where the product undersells itself

1. **The invited-versus-network distinction is the best legal argument available and
   it is a footnote.**
2. **Article 25(4) is never cited** — a real, favourable provision describing
   exactly what RateLoop is.
3. **Article 26(3)'s "freedom to organise its own resources"** is the single best
   citation for outsourcing the performance of oversight. Unused.
4. **The deferral to December 2027 is a selling point, not a problem** — eighteen
   months to build oversight practice and accumulate evidence _before_ the duty
   bites. The docs treat it as a compliance fact rather than a runway.
5. **The candour of the privacy notice is a competitive asset presented as a
   limitation.** Statements that the product does not claim launch-level GDPR
   compliance, does not claim database-level anonymity, and does not market
   attestations it does not hold are markedly more honest than the norm and will
   survive procurement diligence competitors' pages will not. It reads as apology;
   it should read as method.
6. **Blinding and sealed answers map to the automation-bias limb better than any
   first-party tool can.** Independence from the deciding party is a genuine
   methodological advantage, understated as a feature bullet.

## 3. Other exposure

### GDPR

The existing privacy assessment correctly applies the EDPB's blockchain guidelines
(v2.0, adopted 7 July 2026) and is unusually honest: keyed and salted values remain
personal data where a person can be singled out, and the records must never be
described as anonymous.

Its own unresolved points, which remain open:

- **Necessity is undecided** — the controller must still document why on-chain
  settlement is necessary and proportionate, and the assessment itself says the
  public-chain lane stays disabled if a less invasive design would do. Given that a
  conventional off-chain payout ledger plainly achieves reviewer payment, this is a
  genuinely hard test to pass.
- Cross-round unlinkability may not be claimed until per-round payout destinations
  are the enforced default.
- Automated-decision-making analysis is open.
- Ten evidence artefacts are missing, each a blocker — including the signed
  assessment itself, the processing record, the transfer analysis, and counsel
  decisions on lawful bases and residual risk.

**Erasure versus immutability is unresolvable, not merely unresolved.** Keeping
direct identifiers off-chain is the right mitigation, but an erasure request against
on-chain pseudonymous data has no technical remedy. It must be disclosed before the
commit, not after.

Two live inconsistencies: the controller/processor split is described three
different ways across the Terms, the processing agreement and the privacy notice;
and an internal runbook requires disclosing a US email-provider transfer that
neither public page makes.

### Platform Work Directive (EU) 2024/2831

In force 1 December 2024; **transposition due 2 December 2026**.

The paid lane is squarely a digital labour platform, and the directive applies
_"irrespective of the nature of the contractual relationship or the designation of
that relationship by the parties"_ — contractual labelling gives no protection.

**Which duties reach non-employees:** limits on automated monitoring; a
data-protection impact assessment; transparency on automated systems; **human
oversight of automated decisions**; and **human review**.

Two are operational build requirements with deadlines, not transparency
by-products:

- **A decision to restrict, suspend or terminate an account must be taken by a human
  being.** The product currently describes an aggregate finding that _"can pause new
  assignments"_ with an appeal. That is close, but pausing assignments off an
  aggregate score is exactly what the article targets. A human must take the
  decision, not merely hear the appeal.
- **A substantiated reply to a review request within two weeks**, and written
  reasons for payment refusal by its effective date.

**Worker classification is the second gap.** The internal reference derives
behavioural guardrails from a crowdworker ruling but takes no position on bogus
self-employment, and classification is not on the counsel list. The directive's
presumption of employment is its other half.

### Tax, sanctions, payments

**Tax reporting for platform operators** is analysed well internally and I found
nothing to correct: personal services are reportable, the small-transaction
exemption is goods-only, and the declaration is collected at paid-task unlock —
before the first voucher, never as a surprise withdrawal gate. Four questions remain
genuinely open, including treatment of pseudonymous on-chain payees and whether an
unclaimed-but-claimable payout counts as credited. The last is sharpened by the
contract's immutability, since a claim is permissionless and may sit indefinitely.

**Sanctions** bind everyone, with no minimum threshold. Screening at wallet
connection and at eligibility issuance is the right control point because there is
no payout call to screen. **The residual risk is structural**: a reviewer sanctioned
between eligibility and claim can still claim, because the immutable core cannot
stop an already-earned permissionless claim. That is a disclosure, not a fixable
bug — and the public Terms do not currently carry it.

**Crypto-asset rules** probably do not bite: no operator path to user funds, an
adminless escrow, permissionless claims, no pooled balances. But this is a posture
rather than a conclusion — there is no on-point precedent for fee-charging
non-custodial escrow, and **the fee-charging element is the weakest link**, since an
operator taking a percentage of flows it does not custody is the fact pattern
regulators have been most interested in.

**The stablecoin issuer retains pause and blacklist authority over transfers,
including to and from the escrow.** This is disclosed consistently in four internal
places and on three public pages — **the cleanest internal-to-public alignment in
the codebase, and the model for how every other limitation should be disclosed.**

### Two more

**Unfair-competition framing is mandated internally and unimplemented.** An internal
rule requires the product be described as paid panel research and _never_ as
"reviews", because presenting commissioned reviews as consumer feedback is
blacklisted. The entire public surface uses "review" pervasively. The escape — that
the rule targets _consumer_ reviews and this is B2B panel research — is probably
right, but no document reconciles the rule with the shipped vocabulary, and counsel
sign-off is outstanding.

**B2B-only is a hard internal rule and a soft public promise.** The Terms say real-money
flows are _"intended for"_ approved business customers. **"Intended for" does not
switch off consumer law.** If a consumer transacts anyway, withdrawal and
order-button machinery applies — and that machinery is not built. Combined with the
consequence that a mislabelled order button can mean no contract was formed, after
the money has already moved and cannot be recalled, this is material and asymmetric.

## 4. What may be claimed

### Defensible

1. RateLoop produces **signed, tamper-evident records of human review** that a
   deployer can put in its evidence file.
2. **Your designated people can perform their Article 26(2) oversight through
   RateLoop** — the Act says natural persons, not employees, and expressly preserves
   the deployer's freedom to organise its resources.
3. **Independent blinded panels are a strong control against automation bias**, which
   Article 14(4)(b) names explicitly.
4. **Under Article 25(4), providers need written agreements with third-party service
   suppliers — we are one.**
5. **Annex III obligations now apply from 2 December 2027 — that is your runway.**
6. **The public network is an independent quality signal, not your Article 26
   oversight.** Honest, and it protects the customer from a costly mistake.
7. Records are retained for at least six months by default, which **supports but does
   not satisfy** your own retention duties.
8. We do not claim launch-level GDPR compliance; here are the gates still open.
9. The stablecoin issuer can pause or blacklist transfers, including to and from the
   escrow, and our adminless design does not override that.
10. Chain evidence proves the recorded chain facts and nothing more — not source
    quality, reviewer competence, or the merits of your decision.

### To avoid

1. **"Article 14 compliance"**, or any form of "makes you compliant".
2. **"Discharges", "satisfies" or "meets"** an Article 14 or 26 obligation — the Act
   provides no mechanism to transfer deployer status.
3. **Bare requirement citations paired one-to-one with features** — the checklist
   form is an implied compliance claim however the body is hedged.
4. **Any present-tense "verified host" enforcement claim while no host holds the
   tier** — and especially the verb _prove_.
5. **"Codex desktop is the primary verified path"** — contradicted by the product's
   own registry.
6. **Articles 12 and 72 in a deployer-facing mapping.**
7. **The securities record-keeping row.**
8. Anything implying reviewers are **pre-qualified experts** — the Terms already say
   an invitation proves project access and nothing more.
9. **"Anonymous", "unlinkable", or any erasure promise for on-chain data.**
10. **"EU-hosted", "EU-only" or "EU sovereign"** absent dated evidence — account data
    and logs at one provider remain in the US.
11. **"Reviews" as the product noun in paid-lane marketing**, pending counsel
    sign-off.
12. **"Intended for business customers"** as the sole B2B gate — either enforce it or
    build the consumer machinery.
13. **Any general-purpose-AI claim** — those obligations bind model providers, and
    RateLoop trains and places no model.

## 5. Three things for counsel first

1. **The "verified host" language, now.** A capability no host holds is offered as
   the answer to the one Article 14 measure the product cannot otherwise support,
   using the word "prove". Registry-gate the copy or state plainly that no host is
   verified. This is the only item worth treating as urgent.

2. **RateLoop's own high-risk exposure.** The product assigns paid tasks, scores
   reviewer quality, and can pause assignments and suspend accounts. If any of that
   is AI-driven, RateLoop may itself be a deployer — and likely provider — of an
   Annex III point 4 system: task allocation and performance monitoring of people in
   a work-related relationship. The internal note that behavioural scoring stays
   disabled pending an assessment is the right instinct, but it is framed as a data
   protection control rather than an AI Act one. **The product reasons carefully
   about its customers' exposure and has not turned the same lens on itself.** Take
   this together with the Platform Work Directive build requirements and the
   unaddressed classification question.

3. **The AI Act has no internal backing document.** Every other regulatory position
   — tax, sanctions, payments, competition, digital services — has an internal
   reference with an explicit counsel backlog. The AI Act mapping, which is the
   product's primary sales proposition, exists only on marketing pages and is on no
   counsel list. The privacy runbook presumes every public claim has corresponding
   internal documentation and a named review owner. For these claims, that record
   does not exist.
